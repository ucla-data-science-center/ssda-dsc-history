# Deep-research request: the intellectual/field-level lineage of UCLA's data archive

## Project context

I'm writing an intellectual history of UCLA's social science data archive lineage for a
public website: Political Behavior Archive (1961, founded by political scientist Dwaine
Marvick) → founding member of ICPSR (1962) → merged into UCLA's Survey Research Center
(1964) → became an ISSR service unit (1976/77) → UCLA Library (2014) → Data Science Center
(2018). The institutional/administrative facts for this chain are already well-documented
(directors, staff, dates, org changes) through separate research — **that is not what I need
from you.**

**What I need instead:** the *intellectual* history. Why did archives like this one emerge
when they did, what problem were they solving, what disciplinary/methodological currents
produced them, and how does UCLA's specific version of this story compare to peer
institutions founded in the same narrow window? I want to understand the archive as an idea,
not just an org chart.

## What I already know (anchors — don't re-derive, use as context)

- **Peer cohort with founding dates** (from Rice & Southall, *The Data Librarian's Handbook*,
  2016, Table 1.1): Roper Center (1957, USA), Berkeley (1958, USA), GESIS/Central Archive for
  Empirical Social Research (1960, Germany), **UCLA Data Services (1961)**, ICPSR (1962, USA),
  DANS/Steinmetz Archive (1964, Netherlands), Carleton University SSDA (1965, Canada),
  Princeton Data Service (1965, USA), Wisconsin Data & Program Library Service (1966, USA),
  Institute for Policy and Social Research (1966, USA), UK Data Archive (1967), RAND Data
  Library (1968), NARA Electronic Records Division (1968), Odum Archive/UNC (1969).
- **ICPSR founding**: founded 1962 by political scientist Warren E. Miller "to share
  scientific data" — before this, "scientific data from all disciplines were closely held by
  their developers and creators" (ICPSR's own self-description). UCLA was a founding member
  institution. UCLA's own current Dataverse page claims UCLA was one of **8** founding
  members — this specific number is unverified and not something I need you to chase; I'm
  more interested in the founding rationale and cohort composition generally.
- **A hypothesis already floated internally** (from a 2017 internal slide-deck note, not a
  scholarly source — needs testing, not assuming): "Origin of data libraries in the 60s have
  as much to do with centralized computing environments on campuses as social sciences
  developing as empirical research... Data sharing emerged from cost pressure (surveys were
  expensive); the inter-archive network spurred the need for data curation, documentation,
  stable files." I want to know whether this "computing access + survey cost pressure +
  behavioral-revolution empiricism" explanation holds up against actual historical/
  historiographical scholarship, or whether it's an oversimplified retrospective story.
- **Dwaine Marvick** — UCLA political science professor, founded the Political Behavior
  Archive in 1961. I don't currently know his specific scholarly interests, what motivated
  him to start an archive, or whether he was personally involved in ICPSR's national founding
  effort (the Dataverse page's "through his efforts, UCLA was one of the 8 founding members"
  language implies a personal role, unverified).

## What I need you to find (public web sources; scholarly sources preferred where they exist)

1. **Historiography of the social-science data archive movement.** Is there scholarly
   literature (history of science, history of computing, sociology of science, data
   librarianship, or political-science disciplinary history) that treats the 1957–1969
   founding wave above as a coherent intellectual/institutional movement? What causal
   explanation(s) does that literature give — behavioral revolution in political
   science/sociology, rise of survey research as method, foundation/NSF funding priorities,
   access to mainframe computing, cost of primary data collection, something else?
2. **ICPSR's founding rationale in more depth.** What did Warren Miller and the founding
   member institutions say, at the time or in retrospective accounts, about why a shared data
   consortium was needed in 1962? Is there a founding document, charter, or early
   retrospective (10th/25th/50th anniversary essay) that lays out the intellectual case?
3. **Dwaine Marvick's scholarly profile and motivations.** What was Marvick known for as a
   political scientist? Any obituary, festschrift, department history, or biographical
   source describing why he started the Political Behavior Archive or his role (if any) in
   ICPSR's founding? Was "political behavior" as a subfield itself part of the behavioral
   revolution this archive movement is sometimes attributed to?
4. **How peer archives describe their own founding rationale.** Pick at least 3 of the peer
   cohort above (Roper Center, Berkeley, ICPSR, UK Data Archive are good candidates — official
   histories usually exist) and find their own stated founding motivations. Does a common
   pattern emerge, or were the motivations more institution-specific than the "movement"
   framing suggests?
5. **Test the computing-access hypothesis directly.** Find sources (history of computing,
   history of the social sciences) discussing the relationship between mainframe computing
   access on university campuses in the late 1950s/1960s and the emergence of data archives
   specifically — is this a documented causal link in the literature, a plausible but
   undocumented inference, or contested?

## What NOT to do

- Don't re-research ISSR directors, staff, dates, or UCLA org-chart history — that's a
  separate, already-completed research pass. This prompt is about ideas and disciplinary
  context, not UCLA administrative facts.
- Don't try to resolve the "8 founding ICPSR members" number — not the point of this pass.
- Don't fabricate a tidy narrative if the evidence doesn't support one. If the "movement"
  framing turns out to be a retrospective simplification with more institution-specific or
  contingent motivations underneath, say that plainly — that's a more honest and more
  interesting finding than a clean story.

## Output format

For each of the five questions: a short answer, sources cited with URLs (prefer peer-reviewed
or archival sources over blog posts where available), and a confidence note. Flag anywhere
the internally-floated "computing + cost pressure + behavioral revolution" hypothesis is
contradicted, complicated, or unsupported by what you find.
