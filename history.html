From the Political Behavior Archive to the Data Science Center

An institutional history of UCLA’s social science data archive, 1961–present

Author

Tim Dennis

Published

13 September 2026

Introduction

This is the history of one job at UCLA: keeping research data so that people can find it, use it, and still rely on it years later. That job has been done here since 1961, sixty-five years now, under five different institutional homes and at least as many names. It is not a straight line. The unit’s name, reporting line, and formal scope changed again and again. The work did not. A 1961 archive of punch-card survey data and a 2024 platform running petabytes of restricted voter records are recognizably the same work, done now for a much larger campus and with much larger stakes. This account tries to describe each era in its own terms rather than reading today’s vocabulary backward onto it.

A companion document holds the full evidence: the people roster, project inventories, how the charts were built, and the discrepancies that are still open. It is at history-companion.qmd. This document is the narrative.

The history in 30 seconds

  • UCLA’s data archive goes back to 1961, when political scientist Dwaine Marvick founded the Political Behavior Archive.
  • It moved through several institutional homes over six decades. It was never one continuous organization, but the work continued as the campus around it changed: a survey center (1964), a research institute (1974), a formally-named archive within that institute (1977), the same institute renamed (about 2009) and then gone (about 2011), and finally its own unit inside the UCLA Library (2014).
  • Two long-running survey series, LAMAS (1970–1976) and its successor LACSS (1992–2002+), are the closest thing to a continuous data product across that span, with SCSS covering the years between them.
  • One person, Elizabeth “Libbie” Stephenson, is documented on the archive’s staff or running it from the late 1970s (ICPSR’s retirement profile dates her UCLA start to about 1977; other sources document her from 1986) until she retired in 2016. She is the clearest human through-line across the institutional changes.
  • In 2018 the archive’s umbrella became the Data Science Center, which carried the same work to new kinds of research data: 3D and spatial capture, restricted and sensitive datasets, and course material treated as a citable output.

A note on the author’s position

Tim Dennis directed the Social Science Data Archive and the Data Science Center from April 2017 through 2026. This is his account of the archive’s history, written to leave a record of work that ran for six decades and is now being reorganized out of its standalone form. It is a point of view. No institutional history is written from nowhere. The record it rests on is held to a plain standard:

  • Claims about the recent decades are tied to cited institutional records, not to memory. Because the author took part in those years, they are held to a higher documentation standard than the earlier ones, not a looser one.
  • Judgments about what worked and what was lost are marked as judgments.
  • Claims about cause are kept within what the evidence supports.
  • Work done by a team is credited to the team. Individuals are named where authorship, formal authority, or accountability makes it matter.

Evidence cutoff: 2026-08-31. Corrections and version history are in the project repository. More on the sources and their limits is in Methods and limitations.

Institutional timeline

1961-1964: The Political Behavior Archive

Political scientist Dwaine Marvick, who had joined UCLA’s faculty in 1954, founded the Political Behavior Archive (PBA) in 1961.1 Rice and Southall place it in the field’s founding cohort: UCLA’s data service is the fourth-oldest social-science data library in their sample, after the Roper Center (1957), Berkeley (1958), and GESIS (1960), and ahead of ICPSR itself (1962).2 What the PBA did was straightforward for its moment: it distributed survey and behavioral data from ICPR, the Roper Public Opinion Research Center, and local holdings to UCLA researchers.3 A UC memorial calls Marvick “an early advocate and practitioner of computer applications to political data,” which makes founding an archive a reasonable move for him, though no source found so far says why he did it in 1961 in particular. That silence is worth noting. Heinz Eulau’s detailed 1999 memorial covers Marvick’s career closely and never mentions the archive at all.4

The following year, UCLA was among the founding member institutions of ICPSR. This one is confirmed against the primary document: Warren Miller’s August 1962 memo to Rensis Likert making the case for the consortium names the members “as of July 30, 1962,” and “University of California at Los Angeles” is on the list.5 Two things often attached to that fact are shakier, and both come from the same place, the text Libbie Stephenson wrote for the archive’s Dataverse page. It says there were eight founding members and that UCLA joined “through [Marvick’s] efforts.” That is the archive’s own memory, not an independent record. A longtime ICPSR staff member’s list from membership records has 22 original institutions; Miller’s memo lists 21; and the “8” may instead be a “Committee of Eight” whose 1962–63 correspondence sits in ICPSR’s own archive, a founding committee rather than the dues-paying roster. No document found so far, Miller’s memo included, gives Marvick a formal role in the 1962 founding. His documented ICPSR involvement is governing-board service from 1964, then chair of the ICPR Council in 1966–67. What is documented is that Marvick and Miller were close friends and collaborators from the early 1950s at Michigan’s Survey Research Center. That is a plausible route for “through his efforts,” not proof of it.6

1964-1974: The Survey Research Center years

The archive’s next home was UCLA’s Survey Research Center (SRC), formed in 1964, which housed an “Archival Data Library” into which the PBA merged.7 Marvick’s own August 1964 proposal, “Plans for the Development of a Social Science Archival Data Library in the UCLA Survey Research Center”,8 set out the concept directly, though the document itself has not turned up yet (see history-companion.qmd).

It is worth being precise here, because the archive’s founding date gets told several ways and the confusion is avoidable. The archive work itself, taking in the datasets of the tape-sharing and mainframe era, keeping their codebooks, documenting them, and running a reference room where researchers could work out how to use machine-readable data, was a formal part of the SRC from 1964, and of the PBA before that. What did not exist yet was a named unit with its own staff line and consultative services. Stiles and Stephenson say the SRC’s Archival Data Library ran “on a reference-room basis only.” The name and the formal unit come in 1977. The work is older than the name.

One detail here is unresolved. The archive’s own account, the text Libbie Stephenson wrote for its Dataverse page, puts the 1964 SRC arrangement “under the direction of Eve Fielder.” A published obituary says Fielder became SRC’s first director only in 1976. So either SRC ran from 1964 under someone else, or the account compresses two events (see history-companion.qmd).9

The SRC’s main data product during this decade was the Los Angeles Metropolitan Area Surveys (LAMAS): ten waves, 1970–1976, a shared omnibus survey run about twice a year with questions submitted by different researchers.10

The decade’s clearest mark came from outside the archive. Around 1970 the UCLA Library’s Public Affairs Service, under Mary Ryan, bought 1970 census tapes from DUALabs. It was the library’s first move into numeric data, and it was cited nationally as an early case of a library taking on machine-readable data.11

1974-1977: ISSR, and the archive gets a name

The Institute for Social Science Research (ISSR) was established in 1974, taking in the SRC and its archival data library, under founding director Howard E. Freeman, a medical sociologist.12 Three years later, Associate Director Donald Treiman did what several sources describe in different words. He took the archival data library that had run “on a reference-room basis only,” designed its research-support framework (the model of working alongside a project across its whole life cycle that the archive still gives as its mission), and formalized it into a named service unit, the Social Science Data Archive (SSDA), with its own Data Archivist line.13 An OECD international directory dates the “ISSR Data Archive at UCLA” to December 1977, for quantitative research and “primarily secondary analysis.”14

So 1977 is not the archive’s founding. The archive dates itself to 1961, and the work ran without a break through the PBA and the SRC’s Archive Data Library. 1977 is when it was named, given a staff line, and built out from a reference room into a service that consulted. The sources that look like they disagree are describing different parts of that one change. The archive’s own account (Stephenson) credits Treiman with the framework and does not mention ISSR. The 2017 decks and the OECD directory name ISSR as the home. The team’s published book chapter15 says “as part of the Survey Research Center,” which by 1977 was a component of ISSR. What still needs a documentary check is the exact reporting line, whether Treiman’s 1977 framework sat directly under ISSR or under the SRC within ISSR. The year and the continuity are not in question.

One episode from around 1980 runs against the direction most of this history moves in. When the UCLA Library decided not to buy 1980 census tapes, ISSR and SSDA took them on instead, with all the support and storage that came with them.16 Numeric-data stewardship moved from the Library to the Archive, the reverse of what would happen in 2014.

1977-2014: SSDA under ISSR, then ISR, then on its own

The archive’s 37 years under this umbrella were not one arrangement. They fall into three subperiods.17

  • ISSR proper (1977 to the late 2000s). The archive was a service unit of the Institute for Social Science Research. Its directors were Howard E. Freeman (founding director, 1974–1981), Marilynn B. Brewer (from about 1983 until she left UCLA for Ohio State in 1993), and David O. Sears (1993–2008). UCLA’s General Catalogs list SSDA as an ISSR component through at least 2005–07.
  • Institute for Social Research (about 2009 to 2011). By the 2009–10 UCLA General Catalog the parent institute had been renamed ISR, with the same components (including the Data Archive) and a new website. The Survey Research Center is no longer listed among them; it had moved to a Health Services Research division in 2004. No director is named under ISR.
  • Standalone SSDA (about 2011 to 2014). By the 2011–12 catalog neither ISSR nor ISR appears. The Social Science Data Archive is listed on its own, as a Social Sciences collection with its own site (dataarchives.ss.ucla.edu), and stays that way through 2013–14. No source records a formal dissolution of the parent institute or a single successor. The reporting line from here to the 2014 Library transfer is unresolved.

The recurring survey work carried on throughout, renamed and reshaped: the Southern California Social Survey (SCSS), then the Los Angeles County Social Survey (LACSS), which ran at least 1992–2002 (the full series detail is in history-companion.qmd). By the 1990s the archive had become, in its own staff’s words, “the de facto campus-wide data service unit,” serving public health, education, policy studies, law, and management, not only social science departments.18 A surviving SSDA newsletter from about 1979 names Martin Pawlocki (programmer), Gina Jamison (the same person as today’s DSC staff member Jina “Jamie” Jamison), and Shirley Lane, who had come from the UCLA Research Library’s machine-readable 1970 Census project. The newsletter puts the archive in the Gayley Center Building by 1979, after an earlier stint in Franz Hall.19

The clearest human through-line for the whole era is Elizabeth “Libbie” Stephenson. ICPSR’s retirement profile dates her UCLA start to about 1977, the year the archive became a named ISSR unit, and she is documented as staff and later Director through the 2014 transfer, retiring in 2016.2021 Her long run of work on bibliographic control, with Sue Dodd’s scholarship, IASSIST, and ICPSR, is early data-citation practice under an older name: making data properly identified and findable. By 2013, right before the Library transfer, SSDA was already testing the DDI-Lifecycle metadata standard and Colectica curation software. It was moving toward modern research-data practice on its own. The era’s clearest outside connection was across borders: the Intergenerational Stratification and Mobility File (ISMF), built with Harry Ganzeboom of the Free University in Amsterdam and Donald Treiman, and funded by the Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research.22

2014-2018: The Library MOU, and a change of director

The MOU, effective 2014 and executed 2015, moved SSDA from the Social Sciences Division to the UCLA Library. It was the first time the archive left ISSR to stand on its own. It carried 2.0 FTE, about $245K in permanent funds, and about $29K in subscriptions (ICPSR, Roper, UC Berkeley/SDA, Colectica/Algenta), plus a small foundation gift fund; staff who came across included Elizabeth Stephenson and Jina “Jamie” Jamison.23 The stated purpose was to “expand access to the archive’s expertise while modernizing its services,” which reads as a mandate to grow the work, not just move it. The MOU also carried a consultation clause: if the Library later made “substantial changes to the operations of Social Sciences Data Archives,” it was to meet with the Social Sciences Division and keep faculty access intact.

In April 2017 Tim Dennis was hired as Director of SSDA, succeeding Stephenson, whose own tenure reached back to the late 1970s.24 That November he presented “Library Data Archive” to UCLA Library staff, already framing 1961 to 2017 as one continuous arc under the tagline “Providing archive and data services since 1961.”25

The team then reworked the unit’s goals and services using design thinking, with “the user as the scholar in need of research support” at the center.26 In Spring 2018 the result became official: the unit was restructured and renamed the Data Science Center (DSC), with a new visual identity and a mission written to cover any discipline,

to foster a welcoming research community by developing data literacy and foundational coding skills through consulting and education … supporting researchers throughout the full data life cycle: planning and data management plans; acquisition, storage, cleaning and usage; analysis; publication; curation; and preservation.27

SSDA became a named component of it. Per Tim, Todd Grappone, then the Library’s AUL for Digital Initiatives and IT, handled the 2014 transfer, coined the “Data Science Center” name, and helped design the 2018 service model.28

2018-present: The Data Science Center

This is the era with the widest gap between the unit’s size and its reach, and the era this history’s author directed. It is written against institutional records and cited sources rather than recollection, and the judgments in it are marked as judgments. Working notes and the full evidence trail are in evidence/library-era-2014-present.md and history-companion.qmd.

Continuity, not rupture

The 2018 rebrand changed the unit’s name and widened its scope. It did not replace what came before. ucla-data-archive_old, a repository built around the “Data Archive” identity, holding its collections policies, terms, and deposit and preservation practices, was created in July 2017 and actively maintained through May 2020, two years after the unit’s formal name became “Data Science Center.” The archive’s core work, repository hosting, which descends directly from the LAMAS, SCSS, and LACSS series, ran under the new name the whole time. Jina “Jamie” Jamison, who came across with the archive in 2014, still leads that repository work today. She is the one human through-line across the whole Library era.

The growth over that span is easy to state. Staff went from 2.0 FTE at the 2014 transfer (a director and a data archivist) to five by about 2021, mostly by redesigning existing library positions rather than hiring new ones: a geospatial librarian moved in from another department and was rebuilt as a Spatial Data Science Librarian (Zhiyuan Yao), a data manager from the Library’s IT division was rebuilt as a Data Science Facilitator, and a Digital Library programmer with a machine-learning background was recruited across divisions (Kristian Allen, a 0.5-FTE role, 2018–2024).29 UCLA Dataverse went from 9 datasets and 229 downloads in January 2020 to roughly 1,600 datasets and more than 143,000 downloads by 2026.30 The staffing did not grow in proportion. Part of how the unit absorbed the load is the student program described below; part is that some capacity was simply lost, a GIS position cut in 2024 and other roles ended, with the demand in those areas still there.

What stayed constant, and what changed

For six decades and five institutional homes the constant has been the same: keep research evidence findable, usable, and preserved, and extend that to whatever kind of evidence researchers happened to be producing. What changed after 2018 is the range of things that covers. Not only survey datasets now, but restricted and sensitive data that needs governed computation, geospatial data and aerial imagery, 3D and spatial capture, text corpora and computational text analysis, research software, and course material treated as a citable output.31 Look back at that list and it tracks the field’s own sequence, from data citation to open and FAIR data to open science to software curation to computationally intensive research support. That is a way of narrating what changed after the fact, not a plan anyone was working to. The sections below take the main strands one at a time.

The repository: UCLA Dataverse

The legacy SSDA collection had lived on Harvard’s hosted Dataverse. In 2018 the DSC stood up a local UCLA instance, chosen for the same reasons the archive had chosen Dataverse in the first place: DDI metadata, the ability to restrict licensed data, and a curation ladder from self-service deposit to fully mediated. The DSC wired it to UCLA single sign-on, DOI minting, and preservation through the California Digital Library. Research groups run their own branded sub-collections inside it with DSC training. One deliberate choice was to harvest the legacy SSDA collection into the discovery layer rather than migrate it right away: keep it working first, move it later.

The collections DSC hosts now run well past social science. The UCLA Law Behind Bars Data Project covers COVID-19 in prisons, jails, and detention nationally, about 1,169 items. The Williams Institute’s LGBT law-and-policy data, which DSC curation helped bring to ICPSR directly, includes TransPop, the first national probability sample of transgender people in the US. The Cotsen Institute and DIG archaeology collections hold about 6,360 items in DIG alone, more than the whole historical SSDA, LAMAS, SCSS, and LACSS corpus put together. DIG’s own stated aim, to “expand citation practices” in archaeology, is the same bibliographic-control instinct Stephenson brought to the 1980s, applied to a different field at a much larger scale.

Running a repository also meant defending it. In early 2025, bots mass-downloaded UCLA Dataverse content, apparently to train a model, rotating across thousands of addresses to get around IP blocking. DSC identified the pattern and deployed an open-source filter that had it contained by mid-year.32

Governed computation: Redivis and the L2 voter file

Before this, UCLA had no compliant environment for restricted national datasets of this class, the multi-terabyte kind. There was no governed way to give a student access to a voter file, and no secure place to merge FERPA-, PII-, or PHI-restricted data at scale. To support that class of data, DSC selected and licensed Redivis, ran the institutional compliance review (Third-Party Risk Management, P3/P4 approval), negotiated access to the restricted datasets, piloted the platform in 2022, and launched the governed service in January 2023. It set up a 50-seat model with data-use-agreement controls so that routine requests would not pile up on one person.33

The main case is the L2 historic voter file, more than 10 TB of national voter history bought through the archive’s gift fund. It arrived as thousands of fragmented state-level files, too large to store locally and too inconsistent to analyze. DSC pulled it into a single version-controlled national dataset, published with its own DOI (UCLA Library, L2 Voter History and Demographic Dataset, 2023, doi:10.60603/D33W2T) and presented at FORCE11 2024 by Redivis’s CEO alongside the doctoral student who curated it, Graham Straus. Straus put the institutional point plainly: “What happens when I leave UCLA? Will people know how the dataset was constructed? Yes!” Researchers use it directly across departments, in election administration, gentrification, Latino voting, and a project merging the voter file with UCLA medical records to study health and voting. Recorded processing on the platform grew from 444 GB in its first year to 2.9 PB in 2024; those figures measure platform activity, not data held (holdings are about 20 TB). DSC provided the environment and the access. The scholarship stayed with the researchers. The point of it is not an “AI” story. It is that stewardship came to include governed computation, not only acquiring and describing open files.

Teaching at scale, and open science

With two staff and tens of thousands of potential learners, the unit joined The Carpentries in 2017 and used the membership to build an instructor community rather than hire one. Tim founded UCLA Carpentries in 2018 and co-founded the systemwide UC Carpentries network in 2020, when COVID made coordinated cross-campus training necessary. By the 2023–2025 period that network ran 37 workshops across six campuses. As chair of Library Carpentry’s Curriculum Advisory Committee since 2022, Tim moved several lessons into the official worldwide curriculum, among them a Data Management Plans lesson aligned to the NIH data-sharing policy.

The IMLS grant RE-252335-OLS-22 ($237,839, Tim as Project Director)34 treated course material itself as a citable research output: 15 peer-reviewed open-science lessons against 14 proposed, each with a Zenodo DOI, CRediT contributor roles, ORCID identifiers, and Citation File Format metadata, built by 27 authors across 14 teams. It is the clearest case of the software-curation and open-science strands in this era, and, because a federal award can be checked without anyone’s account of it, the one with the firmest outside evidence.

Tim is also Co-PI on the $1.85M Sloan Foundation grant that established the UC OSPO Network, a six-campus effort to treat open-source software as real scholarly infrastructure. That grant funds the network, not DSC. What DSC got out of it is a local UCLA open-source program office, the lead on the network’s education work, and shared services (licensing guidance, a repository browser, a summer student research program). The need is concrete: a 2025 scan of about 81,000 UC-affiliated code repositories found that 77.5% carried no license at all.

Spatial data, 3D capture, and the Lux Lab

Geography has consistently been the unit’s largest single workshop audience. Before the GIS position was cut in 2024, DSC’s geospatial librarian handled 70 to 80 booked consultations a year, started the systemwide UC GIS Week, ran UC GIS workshops that reached hundreds of people, managed the LARIAC regional aerial-imagery holdings (bought through the archive’s gift fund), and worked with UC San Diego and UC Santa Barbara on geospatial discovery for Dataverse. DSC also ran UCLA’s campus ArcGIS Online platform. It grew to more than 7,000 accounts and over 3,000 active users a year, and since the position was cut it has run past its licensed storage with no assigned administrator.35

The clearest case of the archive’s work reaching a genuinely new kind of research object is Doug Daniels and the Lux Lab. Daniels joined the Library in 2013 and the DSC team in 2020 as Emerging Technologies Librarian. He runs UCLA’s point-cloud visualization service (pointcloud.ucla.edu, built on Potree), is FAA-certified for drone work, and has built up a run of 3D-capture projects across archaeology, medicine, and cultural heritage: Easter Island Moai models feeding a Cotsen Institute atlas, cadaver-arm scans now taught in History of Medicine, a 3D-printed surgical guide in use at the medical center, drone-LiDAR surveys in Cambodia and Iceland, and, with DataSquad students and the Vietnamese Heritage Museum, a public 3D narrative of a refugee family’s escape by sea. He was named a UCLA Librarian of the Year for 2026. His work also runs straight back to the donor relationship: he scanned Powell Library itself and showed the lab’s work to the Powell Society donor circle.

DataSquad: a student research workforce

DataSquad, modeled on a program at Carleton College, began in 2020 on a Powell gift that paid the first cohort’s salaries and laptops, with the Library adding funds in 2023 to expand it. It pairs undergraduate and graduate consultants with staff on sustained project work. Reportable project engagements grew from a handful in 2023 to more than a hundred in 2025, as departments started planning research around its availability rather than calling it only when something broke. Of the ten students whose outcomes after graduation were tracked in the 2023–2025 period, all went into data or technical roles, with McKinsey, Amazon, AIG, Stanford, and CVS Health among the destinations. The model has been picked up elsewhere through a DataSquad International network. Its research contributions are in the spotlights under Research impact.

People and funding

Much of this era’s expansion ran on Norman Powell’s philanthropy: a data-scientist position, a deep-learning machine and DSC space in the Young Research Library, and DataSquad’s seed funding, all separate from his dated $5M December 2016 gift that endowed the University Librarian position. A quieter piece of budget work mattered nearly as much. DSC moved the archive’s large recurring subscriptions, ICPSR and Roper, off its own gift fund and onto the Library’s general membership budget, the way research libraries usually treat major database memberships. That freed the archive’s original fund to cover the cloud costs of Dataverse and the point-cloud platform. The move was possible because one role could see the archive’s budget and the infrastructure budget at the same time.36

The people who carried the work were Jamie Jamison (repository and curation), Doug Daniels (3D and emerging technology), Leigh Phan (programmer to data scientist over this period), a GIS/spatial librarian, a data-science facilitator, and the rotating DataSquad students. The full roster, with what is and is not independently documented, is in history-companion.qmd.

Research impact

Two different things get measured here, and they answer different questions.

Historical traces of reuse

Holdings activity shows when the archive’s deposited datasets were originally collected, by decade. It stands in for how busy the archive was. Citation evidence shows which of that data is documented as having been used in later published research. It stands in for downstream value, not for the archive’s own activity. The two do not share a scale and should not be read against each other: a decade of heavy collecting does not have to produce more citations, and citations can arrive, and here do arrive, decades after collection.

Methods and limitations

The author’s position is stated up front, in “A note on the author’s position.” Tim Dennis directed the unit for the recent decades this history covers, and is separately the author of documents (a grant proposal, reorg-response material) that use some of the same facts for advocacy. A repository boundary does not change that. What this project does instead:

  • The author’s role, and this project’s relationship to ospo_datasquad_grant, are stated plainly rather than left for the reader to find.
  • Facts are sourced to a claim register (dsc-evidence-registry), not asserted from memory. See claims/ssda-*, claims/icpsr-*, claims/issr-*, claims/dennis-*, and claims/dsc-* there for what is verified, disputed, or self-reported.
  • Documented facts are kept separate from interpretation. Discontinuities, gaps, and contested points are stated as such rather than smoothed over. See README.md “Known gaps” and history-companion.qmd “Open discrepancies.”
  • ospo_datasquad_grant may cite this project’s evidence, but it is not independent corroboration of a claim that traces back to the same author.
  • Self-reported achievement claims: much of the 2018-present detail comes from Tim’s and Doug’s own internal personnel-review documents. These are treated as discovery sources, not as sole public evidence. Where independent corroboration exists (press coverage, award records, publication venues, grant award pages), the claim is included and the corroboration is what is cited. Where only the review document supports a claim, it is either left out or explicitly attributed as a self-reported account. See history-companion.qmd for what has been held to that standard.
  • The evidence cutoff is 2026-08-31. An outside read for factual support and proportion is optional, at the author’s discretion; if one happens, the reader is named in “A note on the author’s position.”
