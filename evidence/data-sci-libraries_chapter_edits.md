## Abstract {#abstract}

The University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Library Data Science Center (DSC) is a research and education unit supporting faculty, researchers and students through consultation, instruction, co-curricular programming and data infrastructure. It provides a wide range of researcher support and development in data and computationally intensive scholarship, geospatial analysis and emerging technologies. Since 2018, the DSC has developed services that provide education and support for the increasingly complex research landscape. 

This chapter outlines the process we used to create our new services. We give context to our origins as the Social Science Data Archive (SSDA) that provided social science data services at UCLA since the 1970s.  We examine how integrating the SSDA into the Library in 2014 led us to shift our focus toward a service that supports data creation, interpretation and publication regardless of discipline or methodology. We articulate the drivers for change on the UCLA campus that led us to redesign service offerings. We also describe how our involvement with the Carpentries movement expanded our ability to teach data and coding skills. In the chapter, we reflect on the challenges we faced in establishing a service profile that is non-traditional for a library while focusing on building an inclusive community that democratizes data science tools and their research applications. 

## UCLA Context {#ucla-context}

UCLA is a public research institution located in Los Angeles, California. UCLA has a diverse community of scholars that encompasses nearly 30,000 undergraduates pursuing 125 majors, 13,000 graduate students in fifty-nine research programs and over 7,000 faculty members. To support its research activities, the University deployed a department-based research support infrastructure. Research data support has been heavily siloed across campus, depending on when and where departments can access resources to support these endeavors. Several distinct groups have emerged that provide different support layers for other disciplines. For example, researchers in STEM (science, technology, engineering and mathematics) fields have ready access to course-integrated resources in campus units such as the UCLA Collaboratory in the Institute for Quantitative and Computational Biology and the Institute for Digital Research and Education. These institutes have large staffs and support thousands of researchers annually. 

In contrast, departments in social sciences, humanities and arts lack access to similar institutes or infrastructure. However, data-intensive research is a part of nearly every disciplines’ research workflow. This inequitable support across disciplines has led to gaps in research data support across the UCLA campus. Unequal access to these resources exacerbates existing inequities among researchers and prevents undertrained and undersupported researchers from acquiring the technical support and training they need to advance. The UCLA Library sought to bridge this gap by creating the UCLA Data Science Center, formerly known as the Social Science Data Archive.

## Social Science Data Archive  {#social-science-data-archive}

Created in 1977 as part of the UCLA Survey Research Center[^1], the Social Science Data Archive (SSDA) maintained and preserved data produced by and purchased for UCLA researchers. The cornerstone of the SSDA was a commitment to long-term preservation and useability. 

The SSDA was known locally throughout the UCLA and University of California system for its instructional programs. The SSDA routinely taught topics such as data literacy, data management, analytics software (SPSS, SAS, Stata, Job Control Language) and data discovery/reference. The SSDA also provided instructional support for data analysis in course curricula.

In addition to its instructional program, the SSDA provided reference services for data discovery and maintained an in-house library of codebooks and documentation for the archive material. The Archive was well-known for its role as a steward and manager of a diverse range of social science research data. SSDA provided support for social science scholars and those accessing SSDA data as part of their research. 

## Joining the Library {#joining-the-library}

By the 2010s, the SSDA’s service profile of supporting survey research data collection, management and preservation had become too narrow for researchers’ emerging needs. Given the complexity, scale and variety of sources that generate data, researchers needed more help identifying or using suitable tools to work with data at different stages in the research lifecycle. For example, campus researchers increasingly needed help using open source tools, such as R, Python or OpenRefine, to work with messy or inconsistent data and improve and automate their research workflows. As text, image and audio data formats became a growing part of data projects on campus, SSDA could not accommodate these formats in addition to existing social science data collections. Lastly, SSDA’s focus on social science data restricted its ability to respond to and support emerging interdisciplinary data-intensive research patterns. The changes in disciplinary scope and computational needs of research motivated our team toward a more universal approach to data support.

In 2014, the Social Science Data Archive joined the Library as part of an effort to expand access to its expertise in data management and curation while modernizing its services to support current research innovation. This move to the Library reflected the changing research needs on campus. Data usage had moved beyond the bounds of disciplinary specificity into areas not previously anticipated. 

Considering these emerging needs and the goal of providing expanded services to an expanding audience (the UCLA campus community as a whole), the SSDA team and the Library set out to redesign the unit’s goals and vision while continuing the remarkable history and legacy of the SSDA.

## Redesigning Services {#redesigning-services}

Our team adopted design thinking to envision our goals, mission and how we would support the UCLA campus community. As part of this process, we centered the “user” as the scholar in need of research support. This helped us consider the different backgrounds of students, researchers and faculty on a diverse university campus and how to support them in reaching their goals. While scholarship can be fascinating and exciting, we understand that the processes and challenges can also be daunting. 

Taking into account the broad spectrum of experiences across campus allowed our team to define a vision and mission statement, along with a new name, The Data Science Center (DSC):

*The Data Science Center's mission is to foster a welcoming research community by developing data literacy and foundational coding skills through consulting and education. We support researchers throughout the full data life cycle of activities: planning research projects and data management plans; data acquisition, storage, cleaning and usage; analysis; publication; curation; and preservation.*

We envisioned a new brand and identity for how we wanted the UCLA Library, UCLA campus, national and international communities to identify our team based on our newly-defined mission. In addition to redesigning our services, the DSC also adopted a new visual identity, including a professionally-designed new logo to convey our approach to data science for current research as welcoming, inviting and forward-thinking. We formalized these changes in Spring 2018 by restructuring the Social Science Data Archive as a component of our unit and renamed our organization the “Data Science Center,” reflecting our services’ interdisciplinary scope.

Guiding and assisting researchers with the challenges associated with data-intensive research formed the core of our service. We started to support data processing and analytic areas, such as data cleaning, data visualization, coding help and research method support such as text and cluster analysis. The Data Science Center’s new vision also included becoming a member of The Carpentries, a non-profit volunteer-led data science teaching organization. As part of the Carpentries we sought to recruit a community of data instructors and offer an education based on The Carpentries’ inclusive teaching pedagogy. The DSC would continue to provide core SSDA services of education and consulting in data literacy, data management and data discovery, but would also support researchers and scholars throughout the UCLA campus community in a broader range of data and computational support issues.  

The DSC now engages with a wide range of disciplines, serving over 125 undergraduate majors and 80 graduate and professional programs.

## Using The Carpentries Model {#using-the-carpentries-model}

Initially, the DSC set out to provide data instruction to UCLA’s tens of thousands of scholars with a limited staff of two. We used our membership to The Carpentries to grow an instructor community at UCLA to meet this challenge. In line with our mission of creating an open and inclusive environment for anyone to learn data science skills, the DSC adopted a Code of Conduct modified from The Carpentries’ Code of Conduct.

The Carpentries open source, community-developed curricula allowed us to scale data science instruction to the entire research university community. This model fits well in a federated environment like UCLA because it builds connections with learners while simultaneously recruiting instructors across campus. 

Since 2017, The Carpentries membership at UCLA has grown to over ten instructors across six departments. This growth includes the entire DSC team who have all become Carpentries Instructors. Pedagogically, the foundational data- and software-related lessons meshed well with scholars' needs. The discipline-agnostic nature of many of the lessons attracts learners from diverse academic fields at UCLA. Inside the Library, we have used the Library Carpentry lesson program to teach librarians and information professionals data and coding skills. This curriculum improves the Library’s ability to work effectively with researchers on their current research needs involving data-intensive scholarship. 

In addition to offering consulting and instruction through workshops open to the campus community, the DSC provides data literacy instruction to multiple undergraduate-level programs, including the Sustainable LA Grand Challenge Undergraduate Research Scholars Program, a year-long campus-sponsored research program and the Mellon Mays Undergraduate Fellowship Program. Through word-of-mouth and relationship-building, DSC has also established partnerships with graduate-level and professional programs by providing domain-specific instruction and consultations for multiple programs including the Masters in Social Science, Masters in Urban & Regional Planning, Masters of Applied Statistics and Anderson School of Management programs.

## Data Publishing with UCLA Dataverse {#data-publishing-with-ucla-dataverse}

As part of DSC’s service redesign, we identified a campus need for a local repository to support researchers’ data sharing and publishing needs. Before joining the Library, the SSDA had maintained and preserved its data collections using the Dataverse repository hosted by Harvard University. Dataverse was chosen because it supports the Data Documentation Initiative (DDI) metadata schema, restricts licensed data and provides a tiered curation approach from self-service deposit to fully mediated curation. Additionally, Dataverse is a robust open-source project supported by developers and users active through a worldwide community. This community enables a collaborative development process that unites researchers, data curators and developers in the development of open data practices for data publishing. 

In 2018, the DSC implemented a local instance of Dataverse for the UCLA community. UCLA Dataverse allows researchers to publish and track research data, text, software, scripts and data visualizations created from research projects. Data in the UCLA Dataverse can be made publicly available or restricted based on license requirements, but information about the data remains discoverable, citable and linkable. The Data Science Center sponsors research groups, centers, schools and departments to create their own branded repositories within the UCLA Dataverse to self-administer their data with guidance and training from DSC staff. 

## Staffing and Roles {#staffing-and-roles}

Staffing a new data science unit is a challenge, as the discipline and literature on best practices is still evolving. The DSC staff at UCLA has grown from two initial members to five full-time employees (FTEs) with access to a pool of student employees. The DSC’s initial core members had a background in data librarianship and information science, providing a foundation for data collection, storage, archiving and stewardship. DSC staff built on this core knowledge area and expanded specialization with each additional team member, designing new positions to focus on areas the DSC planned to support in the future. As we added team members, we targeted core support areas, including GIS, augmented reality/virtual reality (AR/VR) and machine learning/artificial intelligence. When adding roles to the DSC we focused less on specific degrees, such as Computer or Library Science and more on candidate knowledge and interest in their skill areas. 

As mentioned previously, the SSDA had two staff when it joined the library \-- a director and a data archivist. However, to accomplish the goals of broadening our support portfolio to all of campus, this proved insufficient. We identified gaps in expertise in providing research support in geographic information systems (GIS), computational skills and machine learning/artificial intelligence. We also determined that we needed more instructors to deliver data science skills-based instruction. 

To address gaps in GIS and computational driven scholarship support, we worked with our library administration to add two data-focused staff members from other library units to the DSC. One was a Geospatial Resources Librarian position previously reporting to a different department. The other was a data manager role that was previously part of our peer unit in the Digital Initiatives and Information Technology (DIIT) division in the library. We then redesigned these jobs into roles (Data Science Facilitator and Spatial Data Science Librarian) to include skills appropriate for data science and more computational support areas. We also supported DSC staff development and reskilling in needed data science tools and methods for these roles by supporting training in data science educational opportunities and professional development.  We worked with other DIIT functional heads to recruit skilled IT staff interested in consulting and teaching for DSC. Fortunately, a programmer in our Digital Library with a background in data science and machine learning became a vital part of the DSC team through these efforts.  

A full-time employee with special expertise is not always required to support a particular subject effectively. Academic units often have access to student hires and they provide a valuable resource for research support. Incorporating student employees into a Data Science unit brings knowledge of current data science tools and classroom techniques to the service. DSC staff can provide mentorship, guidance and opportunities. We have sought out students who can fill our service gaps in the DSC. For instance, we have hired students strong in statistics to bolster our service in that area. The DSC is also currently adopting the DataSquad model for developing student-staffed data consultation programming. Developed at Carleton College, this program model (Lackie, 2019\) helps develop an identity and structure around a student-led data program. Structured appropriately, senior student staff members can recruit and train junior members, freeing up the staff members’ time by taking on some of the routine tasks. 

As mentioned previously, to build instructional and pedagogical skills capacity, the DSC joined the Carpentries in 2017 and started to establish a local instructor community inside the library and on campus. Since joining, we have added thirteen active instructors through DSC’s Carpentries program. Six of those instructors are from the Library and represent technical roles, such as programmer, digital asset coordinator, technical product owner, etc. In developing our instructional program, we found that recruiting from specialized units in the library proved mutually beneficial both for the library’s technical staff and the DSC. Teaching for the DSC was an attractive opportunity for programmers and other staff who had a passion for teaching and sharing their knowledge. In addition to partnering with library staff, we also recruited instructors from other units on campus, such as UCLA’s research computing group, who also provide technical research support to the campus community. 

Through this inclusive instructional approach, DSC has trained librarians and library school students software and data science skills to foster technical competence across the library as a whole. These training sessions help us work more effectively with the Library’s User Engagement division, the largest scholar-facing unit in the library.

### DSC Values

In addition to the targeted subject matter background for each role at the DSC mentioned above, quality data services requires a collection of essential interpersonal skills, including:

* Commitment to diversity, equity and inclusion  
* Empathy for patrons, learners and team members  
* Desire and interest in teaching and instruction  
* Knowledge and interest in software development and programming

We recognize that many of the tools and methods utilized in data science are new. It will be rare for any individual with a particular degree or background to possess expertise in all areas. Therefore, the DSC is committed to personal development and learning to develop a strong team over the long term. Our team’s support for the principle of “being comfortable not knowing the immediate solution to a problem” and granting time for learning and experimentation fosters a safe environment permitting team members to skill-up as needed.

## Future activities  {#future-activities}

The DSC promotes data literacy and functional coding skills across the campus. The center fosters an active and dynamic research community by providing workshops, consulting, research support, collaboration and outreach activities (e.g., GIS Days). However, growing data diversity, emerging interdisciplinary studies and an evolving data landscape raise new Data Science Center challenges. Keeping current with with data-centric methods and adapting to changes in research practice, DSC plans to meet the emerging needs of the UCLA community by following these aspirational goals:

1. Keep pace with cutting-edge techniques and skills to analyze various types of data  
2. Democratize data and data science techniques for researchers and students through inclusive instruction  
3. Make library data collections more accessible for reuse

### Keeping Pace with Techniques and Skills {#keeping-pace-with-techniques-and-skills}

Recent years have witnessed an unprecedented increase in the volume, variety and velocity of data from different sources [(Miller and Goodchild, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=p6Gf1Z). Advances in data collection tools and computational capacity substantially expand the volume and types of available datasets that can be utilized to generate insights. Datasets are no longer limited to numbers but extend to text, video, audio, images and 3D. Meanwhile, with the growing abundance of geospatial information, an increasing number of datasets incorporate geospatial locations with other data attributes. For example, geospatial sites can be static in the short-term, such as a bus stop location, or dynamic, such as a moving vehicle. The integration of geospatial information with text, video, audio and images (i.e., geotagged tweets) provides opportunities to generate valuable spatial and temporal insights. However, working with geospatial data can be challenging due to its complex structure. DSC is working to respond to these changes by providing expertise and support for working with diverse formats (geospatial and 3D) and computational-intensive methodologies (machine learning and text mining). 

### Democratizing Data  {#democratizing-data}

As data plays an increasing role in research, experiments and scientific studies, the ability to understand, construct and analyze data is essential to scholarship and research projects. To promote data literacy, DSC seeks to expand access to data science techniques through inclusive workshops, consultations, group activities and documentation. 

Workshops provide an opportunity to promote basic data analysis techniques to a broad audience without assuming any previous knowledge or data expertise. By focusing on core data science tools, like OpenRefine, git and programming languages including R and Python, DSC workshops provide a solid foundation for learners who are new to the field.

Consultations provide an opportunity to meet with researchers individually or as a group and provide advice that reflects the level of need. Patrons can bring their data and consult about different data processing and analysis options. Holding data-related group activities is another way to promote data literacy. For example, DSC has offered Tidy Tuesday, a weekly social data cleaning lab, designed to facilitate collaborative data skills building. Through activities, DSC promotes data literacy and builds a community of learners with similar interests. For instance, we worked with a Business School student group to organize and judge an inaugural datathon in Spring 2020\. More recently, the DSC collaboratively programmed a UC-wide Love Data Week with over 25 separate workshops and events. DSC will hold more activities such as data wrangling days or hackathons to construct a friendly environment to continue to learn and practice outside of our instructional workshops. 

Finally, the DSC plans to promote data science education by capturing and sharing typical data consultations we support. For example, we recently worked with a graduate student to analyze ancient Buddhist texts with natural language processing techniques. By documenting these types of research, the DSC raises awareness of the possibilities of data science while expanding awareness of our services.

### Accessible Collections  {#accessible-collections}

A critical component in developing widespread data literacy is access to publicly available research data. This access facilitates instruction by providing relevant examples to the diverse communities represented at UCLA. DSC actively engages in the UC-wide Common Knowledge Groups (e.g., data curation, mapping and 3D) to communicate and collaborate with peers in the UC system to promote and facilitate users’ access to datasets. To support access, DSC is engaged in collecting, describing and preserving data in UCLA Dataverse, a data publishing platform that provides mechanisms to cite, identify and download datasets. We are currently partnering with campus departments and individual researchers to curate and share their research outputs in Dataverse. Inside the Library, we are working with the Library collections group to use Dataverse to manage discovery and access to data the library purchases.  

Another specific example of DSC’s activity to provide better access to data is through its work with geospatial data. As part of supporting a dynamic scholarly community, the DSC collects, describes and curates extensive geospatial research outputs and purchased data in UCLA Dataverse. Though Dataverse provides access, basic discovery and persistent identifiers for these data, it does not provide location-enabled search for geospatial data. Working with UC San Diego and UC Santa Barbara, we are expanding DSC's Dataverse service by developing a geospatial discovery system to efficiently share and find geospatial data with the larger academic community. 

In conclusion, this chapter traced how the UCLA Library Data Science Center evolved from a social science data archive into an interdisciplinary data science support unit for campus researchers. By responding to changes in patterns of digital scholarship on campus and reorienting the service to be more user-focused, the DSC broadened support for data at UCLA. By using the network model and inclusive teaching practices provided by the Carpentries’ community-based model, we expanded our capabilities to train a wider audience in computing and data literacy skills. Finally, our staffing model emphasized the development of data skills allowing the DSC to draw on existing staff and promote their career growth. We did this through inclusive, empathic and educational enthusiasm for the service and its goal of improving data-intensive research outputs. Looking forward, we want to be an integral part of democratizing and adopting novel techniques for data scholarship. We want to enable the building of open, accessible and actionable data collections for research and learning at UCLA.
