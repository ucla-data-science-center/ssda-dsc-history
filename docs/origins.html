Origins (1961–1977)

The Political Behavior Archive, 1961

UCLA’s data archive lineage begins with Dwaine Marvick, a UCLA political science professor who founded the Political Behavior Archive (PBA) in 1961. Rice & Southall (2016), The Data Librarian’s Handbook, Table 1.1, situates it in the field’s founding cohort: “UCLA Data Services, 1961” ranks as the fourth-oldest social-science data library or service in their sample, after Roper Center (1957), Berkeley (1958), and GESIS (1960), and ahead of ICPSR itself (1962).

Why archives like this one emerged

UCLA’s archive is one local expression of a broader postwar development in empirical social science. It wasn’t the product of a single, unified “movement” with one agreed rationale. It’s better described as a family of related infrastructure-building projects, and contemporaries were already discussing them comparatively by the mid-1960s: Ralph Bisco’s 1966 review in the American Political Science Review treats Roper, Berkeley, Michigan, and European archives as one emerging domain.

The multi-causal explanation that survives scrutiny: the growth of survey research and behavioral political science produced large bodies of reusable observations. The expense of new surveys created an incentive to preserve and share existing ones. Punch cards, tabulating equipment, and later magnetic tape made large-scale reuse technically possible. Foundations, federal agencies, and membership consortia made the resulting infrastructure sustainable. Training new quantitative researchers wasn’t incidental either. At ICPSR especially, it was a founding purpose, not a service layered on afterward.

One correction to how this was framed internally: centralized campus computing is better documented as an enabling condition and a bottleneck than as the originating cause. The clearest founding documents (ICPSR’s 1962 memorandum, Roper’s founding rationale, the UK Data Archive’s founding committee) foreground research demand, cost, and coordination problems. Computing shows up as the means the solution operated through, not the reason the problem became urgent.

That framing holds up against the actual founding document. Warren Miller’s August 1962 memo to ISR director Rensis Likert, laying out the case for what became ICPSR, opens with the research problem, not a technology problem: “The rapid increase in scholarly interest in the kinds of data which survey research can provide has far outstripped the availability of the survey mechanism to the interested scholar. This hiatus has become a matter of serious concern to many social scientists.” Computing gets no mention in his stated rationale at all.

A founding member of ICPSR, 1962

UCLA was among the founding member institutions of the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR), established in 1962 by political scientist Warren E. Miller to solve an organizational bottleneck in the production of empirical political science. Heavily demanded election-study data needed permanent preservation and dissemination, but so did training new researchers to use it and coordinating a scattered research community. Its founding memorandum set four objectives, conceived together rather than as separable services: developing data resources, formal quantitative training, stimulating new research, and a clearinghouse on ongoing work.

UCLA’s current Dataverse collection page credits this “through [Marvick’s] efforts” and states UCLA was one of 8 founding members. The full institutional membership at founding was larger than 8: Erik Austin, a longtime ICPSR staff member, compiled a list of 22 original institutions from membership records, Michigan plus 21 others. Miller’s own August 1962 memo goes further: it names the members directly, dated “as of July 30, 1962,” and lists 21, “University of California at Los Angeles” among them. Either way, UCLA’s founding-member status is confirmed against the actual 1962 document, not just a later recollection.

That looked like the end of the “8” question, and then it got more interesting. ICPSR’s current director, Maggie Levenstein, used the same “8” figure independently during a UCLA visit, not obviously copying UCLA’s own page. And ICPSR’s own institutional archive at the Bentley Historical Library, per its finding aid, holds a folder titled “Committee of Eight, Correspondence and Minutes, 1962-1963.” That’s a plausible, specific referent for “8” that has nothing to do with the 21/22-institution membership count: a smaller founding committee, not the full roster of dues-paying members. If that’s right, both numbers could be correct, describing two different things. Not confirmed. Nobody has read that folder yet.

WarningThe headcount is fixed. The personal-role claim is now plausible, still not proven.

UCLA’s founding-member status is confirmed against the primary 1962 document itself. The personal-role question moved, but didn’t resolve. No source found so far, including Miller’s own founding memo, gives documentary evidence Marvick had a formal role in founding ICPSR in 1962. ICPSR’s first annual report names its initial council members, and he isn’t on it. But a 1999 memorial by Heinz Eulau, a colleague of both men, establishes something new: Marvick and Warren Miller were personal friends and research collaborators a full decade before either the archive or ICPSR existed, together at Michigan’s Survey Research Center in the early 1950s. That’s a real, documented channel through which “through his efforts” could plausibly be true, even though nobody has yet found a document that says so directly. His formally documented ICPSR role remains governing-board service starting 1964, rising to chair of the ICPR Council in 1966-67 (per Eulau, more precise than earlier sources), all after founding, not during it. “Marvick helped connect UCLA to the emerging ICPSR network, likely through his friendship with Miller” is now a defensible sentence. “Through his efforts, UCLA was one of the founding members” still isn’t, on direct documentary evidence. Marvick’s own scholarly profile

Apart from the ICPSR question, Marvick’s documented research program explains why an archive like this would plausibly come from him specifically: survey methods brought from Michigan in 1954, statistical approaches to political behavior, and, per a UC memorial, work as “an early advocate and practitioner of computer applications to political data.” His papers include election surveys, computer codebooks, and printouts spanning Los Angeles politics, the Detroit Area Study, West German elections, and Indian electorate surveys.

Eulau’s 1999 memorial fills in the earlier chapter of that career. Marvick arrived at Michigan’s Survey Research Center in the early 1950s, fresh off a Columbia PhD, as a Carnegie Fellow, working alongside Morris Janowitz on a restudy of the Center’s 1952 election data. Warren Miller was there too, still finishing his own PhD. The two became close, personally and professionally, years before either left Michigan, Miller for a stint at Berkeley, Marvick for UCLA, where Eulau writes he “took deep Western roots.” By 1956 Marvick was already organizing national conversations in the field: he named the three panels for the first APSA meeting sessions devoted exclusively to political behavior. He chaired APSA’s own 1972 annual meeting program at Eulau’s request, and in 1988 spent two days interviewing Eulau for the APSA oral history project, eight double-sided tapes, at his home in Los Angeles.

NoteWhat’s still not documented: why 1961, specifically

No source found so far, including Eulau’s detailed personal memoir, states Marvick’s own reason for founding the archive in 1961. That absence is notable on its own: Eulau’s memoir covers Marvick’s career in real depth, APSA leadership roles, ICPR council chairmanship, his broad intellectual range, his family, and never once mentions the archive. The scholarly profile above makes founding an archive a plausible, well-contextualized move for him, but his personal motivation remains an inference, not a documented fact. His own papers (correspondence, project records from roughly 1959 to 1964) are still the likeliest place that would actually resolve this. Marvick died of a stroke in late February 1999, four weeks after Miller; any direct account would have to come from those papers or from people who knew him, not from him.

Merger into the Survey Research Center, 1964 (disputed)

Three internal sources (two 2017 slide decks and the Dataverse About page) agree that the archive merged into UCLA’s Survey Research Center (SRC) in 1964, “under Eve Fielder.” One deck adds detail: the PBA “merged with a planned Archive Data Library the SRC was to house.”

WarningOpen conflict: was Fielder SRC’s director from 1964?

A published obituary for Eve Fielder states she became SRC’s first director in 1976, the same year external evidence (UCLA’s General Catalog) places SRC’s incorporation into ISSR. This directly conflicts with the “1964, under Fielder” framing repeated across three internal sources. Two readings are possible: SRC existed from 1964 under a different, unidentified founding director and Fielder took over later, or the “1964 + Fielder” framing in the internal decks compresses two separate events into one. Not resolved. See Sources & Open Questions.

Toward 1977

What’s well-established: by the mid-1970s the archive was operating inside SRC, and by 1976–77 it was reconstituted as a service unit of the Institute for Social Science Research (ISSR). Exactly how SRC’s 1976 incorporation into ISSR relates to the archive’s own December 1977 establishment as a named ISSR unit is covered in the next chapter, The ISSR Era.
