# Validation request: oral history outreach vs. newsletter-hunting sequencing

## Project context

I'm writing an intellectual history of UCLA's social science data archive
lineage, for a public Quarto site. The arc: the Political Behavior Archive
(founded 1961 by political scientist Dwaine Marvick) → became a founding
member of ICPSR in 1962 → came under the university's Institute for Social
Science Research (ISSR) starting 1977 → transferred to the UCLA Library via
MOU in 2014 (executed 2015) → became the Data Science Center (DSC) in 2018 →
present day. This is an internal institutional-memory project, not a grant
pitch or advocacy document — the goal is an accurate, citable historical
record, independent of any document that merely references parts of it.

Audience: primarily internal (UCLA Library, DSC staff/successors), secondarily
anyone researching the history of academic social-science data archives. Not
under any deadline pressure; this is a background project run alongside other
work.

## What's already gathered (solid ground, 1961-1977 and 2014-present)

- A 2017 internal deck transcript giving the 1961→1962→1977→2014→2018
  timeline at a high level (dates and named milestones, not detailed
  narrative)
- Rice & Southall (2016), a peer-reviewed scholarly source situating "UCLA
  Data Services (1961)" in the founding cohort of social science data
  archives alongside Roper Center (1957), Berkeley (1958), ICPSR (1962), UK
  Data Archive (1967), Odum (1969) — external, citable, credible
- ICPSR's own history documentation confirming UCLA as a 1962 founding member
- The 2014 SSDA→Library MOU (executed Aug 2015): terms were 2.0 FTE + ~$245K
  + ~$29K subscriptions transferred, plus an SSD consultation clause
- Adjacent project material (grant proposal, reorg-response docs) covering
  2018-present in detail already, just not yet assembled into standalone
  narrative form

## The actual gap

**1977-2014 (the ISSR era, ~37 years) is currently a single unsourced
sentence: "SSDA was under ISSR starting 1977."** No directors, no staff, no
descriptions of what the archive did or how it changed under ISSR
stewardship, no primary documents from that period at all. This is the
weakest part of the timeline by a wide margin — everything before and after
it has multiple independent sources; this stretch has zero.

## New information just surfaced, not yet acted on

I was told old SSDA/ISSR newsletters exist somewhere in physical form but are
**not digitized, and their location is currently unknown** (could be UCLA
University Archives, could be a departing/retired staff member's files,
could be in ISSR's own retained records if ISSR still exists in some form —
unconfirmed).

## The decision I need help stress-testing

I was defaulting toward oral history outreach (contacting former SSDA/ISSR
staff, especially anyone from the 1977-2014 stretch, while they're still
reachable) as the next concrete step to close the ISSR-era gap. But the
newsletter lead changes the picture: newsletters, if located, would be
contemporaneous primary documents — potentially more reliable than
decades-old personal recollection, and they don't require coordinating other
people's time or navigating any consent/privacy considerations.

**Question: should newsletter-hunting/digitization come before oral history
outreach, or should they run in parallel? What am I not weighing correctly?**

Specifically challenge me on:

1. **Sequencing bias** — Am I underrating the "staff availability/mortality
   risk" argument for oral history (people age out, memory fades, someone
   could die or become unreachable) against the "primary source is more
   reliable than memory" argument for newsletters? Which risk is actually
   larger here, and does it change if newsletter-hunting alone could take
   months (unknown archive location) versus oral history outreach which can
   start with a single email?
2. **Consent/IRB scope** — This is an internal institutional history, not
   academic research for publication in a journal. Am I wrong to assume no
   IRB review is needed? What's the actual line between "oral history for an
   institutional record" and "human subjects research" that would trigger
   review requirements, and does it depend on whether I intend to quote
   people by name versus paraphrase?
3. **Effort vs. payoff** — Newsletters are a bounded, one-time location
   problem (find them once, then they're a stable source). Oral history is
   an ongoing relationship-management problem (scheduling, follow-up,
   transcription, fact-checking with the interviewee) that scales with each
   additional person contacted. Given this is a background project with no
   deadline, is "cheapest reliable source first" the right heuristic, or is
   that a trap when the cheap source (newsletters) has unknown lead time to
   even locate?
4. **What would make this the wrong call entirely** — Is there a scenario
   where oral history outreach should NOT wait on newsletters at all — e.g.,
   if the newsletters, once found, are unlikely to cover the interpretive
   "why" (why ISSR took it over, why it left ISSR for the Library) that only
   people who lived it can supply, making the two sources complementary
   rather than substitutable?

Give a confidence score (0-100%) on your recommended sequencing, and be
concrete: what should the first action actually be this week, not a
general principle.
