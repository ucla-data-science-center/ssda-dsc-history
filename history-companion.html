Full 1977-2014 detail cut from the main narrative

The three subperiods (grounded in the digitized UCLA General Catalogs 2003–05 through 2013–14, per evidence/deep-research-report.md; Sears’s dates independently confirmed): - ISSR proper, 1977 – late 2000s. An OECD international directory dates the “ISSR Data Archive at UCLA” to December 1977, for quantitative research “primarily secondary analysis,” drawing on UCLA’s ICPSR membership plus federal statistical agencies (Census, NCHS, NCES, BLS) and local studies like LACSS. - Institute for Social Research (ISR), ~2009–2011. Parent institute renamed by the 2009–10 UCLA General Catalog, same phone, same components including the Data Archive, new website (isr.ucla.edu). Still in the 2010–11 catalog. No director identified under this name. - Standalone SSDA, ~2011–2014. By the 2011–12 catalog neither ISSR nor ISR appears; SSDA is listed independently as a Social Sciences collection with its own site (dataarchives.ss.ucla.edu). No public source names a formal dissolution of the parent or a single successor. The reporting line from here to the 2014 Library transfer is unresolved. - Immediately pre-transfer (2013), SSDA was piloting the DDI-Lifecycle metadata standard and Colectica curation software. - Southern California Social Survey (SCSS), confirmed running 1984-1991 in the Harvard Dataverse SSDA collection (true start year not pinned down, likely closer to 1977-1980, never deposited in ICPSR at all). - 1988 technical infrastructure detail: mainframe (IBM 3090, MVS/VM-CMS), tape management (DTA/DTB numbering system), UCLA’s ORION catalog, ICPSR’s SPIRES search facility, a microcomputer-based (dBase III) tape-tracking system Pawlocki and Stephenson built.1 - Rotating annual LACSS PIs: Lawrence Bobo (1992, then UCLA Assoc. Full visualization methodology

Continuity, not rupture

The 2018 rebrand changed the unit’s name and widened its scope. It did not replace what came before. ucla-data-archive_old, a repository built around the “Data Archive” identity, holding its collections policies, terms, and deposit and preservation practices, was created in July 2017 and actively maintained through May 2020, two years after the unit’s formal name became “Data Science Center.” The archive’s core work, repository hosting, which descends directly from the LAMAS, SCSS, and LACSS series, ran under the new name the whole time. Jina “Jamie” Jamison, who came across with the archive in 2014, still leads that repository work today. She is the one human through-line across the whole Library era.

The growth over that span is easy to state. Staff went from 2.0 FTE at the 2014 transfer (a director and a data archivist) to five by about 2021, mostly by redesigning existing library positions rather than hiring new ones: a geospatial librarian moved in from another department and was rebuilt as a Spatial Data Science Librarian (Zhiyuan Yao), a data manager from the Library’s IT division was rebuilt as a Data Science Facilitator, and a Digital Library programmer with a machine-learning background was recruited across divisions (Kristian Allen, a 0.5-FTE role, 2018–2024).3 UCLA Dataverse went from 9 datasets and 229 downloads in January 2020 to roughly 1,600 datasets and more than 143,000 downloads by 2026.4 The staffing did not grow in proportion. Part of how the unit absorbed the load is the student program described below; part is that some capacity was simply lost, a GIS position cut in 2024 and other roles ended, with the demand in those areas still there.

What stayed constant, and what changed

For six decades and five institutional homes the constant has been the same: keep research evidence findable, usable, and preserved, and extend that to whatever kind of evidence researchers happened to be producing. What changed after 2018 is the range of things that covers. Not only survey datasets now, but restricted and sensitive data that needs governed computation, geospatial data and aerial imagery, 3D and spatial capture, text corpora and computational text analysis, research software, and course material treated as a citable output.5 Look back at that list and it tracks the field’s own sequence, from data citation to open and FAIR data to open science to software curation to computationally intensive research support. That is a way of narrating what changed after the fact, not a plan anyone was working to. The sections below take the main strands one at a time.

The repository: UCLA Dataverse

The legacy SSDA collection had lived on Harvard’s hosted Dataverse. In 2018 the DSC stood up a local UCLA instance, chosen for the same reasons the archive had chosen Dataverse in the first place: DDI metadata, the ability to restrict licensed data, and a curation ladder from self-service deposit to fully mediated. The DSC wired it to UCLA single sign-on, DOI minting, and preservation through the California Digital Library. Research groups run their own branded sub-collections inside it with DSC training, and the Library uses it to manage discovery and access for data it buys.6 One deliberate choice was to harvest the legacy SSDA collection into the discovery layer rather than migrate it right away: keep it working first, move it later.

Growth came in three steps. Late 2021 was the institutional scale-up, when schools and centers began running their own collections. Mid to late 2022 brought the legacy SSDA holdings into the local discovery layer, along with wider campus adoption. From 2023 on the growth was steady. By January 2026 the repository had 135 collections (92 published), 312 user accounts, and about 54,700 released files. The day-to-day work was Jamie Jamison’s. She wrote the preservation documentation, represented UCLA at the 2021 Dataverse Community Meeting, and kept the continuity guarantees working: automated nightly database dumps to cloud storage, consolidated storage buckets, current certificates, and a tested scripted install so the system could be rebuilt if it had to be. She walked depositors through sub-collections, DOIs, and archiving, handled access to the LARIAC imagery, and when Redivis arrived became the first approver for restricted-data requests. She is named as data manager on the published L2 voter dataset.

The collections DSC hosts now run well past social science. The UCLA Law Behind Bars Data Project covers COVID-19 in prisons, jails, and detention nationally, about 1,169 items. The Williams Institute’s LGBT law-and-policy data, which DSC curation helped bring to ICPSR directly, includes TransPop, the first national probability sample of transgender people in the US. The Cotsen Institute and DIG archaeology collections hold about 6,360 items in DIG alone, more than the whole historical SSDA, LAMAS, SCSS, and LACSS corpus put together. DIG’s own stated aim, to “expand citation practices” in archaeology, is the same bibliographic-control instinct Stephenson brought to the 1980s, applied to a different field at a much larger scale.

Reuse is harder to count than deposits. Of about 959 indexed datasets, 180 carry a link to a publication, and 13 of those papers can be traced in OpenAlex, with 258 citations between them. That is a floor, not an estimate. A DataCite export bug drops links, restricted and draft datasets are left out, archaeology datasets tied to books have no DOI to follow, and about 200 public datasets have no publication link on file. The traceable depositors include a radiation-belt modeling group in Earth, Planetary, and Space Sciences led by Yuri Shprits that deposits again and again (four linked papers, 95 citations).

Running a repository also meant defending it. In early 2025, bots mass-downloaded UCLA Dataverse content, apparently to train a model, rotating across thousands of addresses to get around IP blocking, from about April to July. Tim identified an open-source bot filter, Anubis, as the fix, and Jamison put it into production and tuned it, with host-level blocking alongside, so that abusive traffic was stopped without locking out researchers. The attack was contained by summer with no loss to the collection.7

Consulting: from reference desk to research partner

The archive had always answered questions: data discovery, codebook lookups, help with SPSS, SAS, and Stata. The 2018 redesign kept that and widened it. The DSC’s new mission statement, written through a design-thinking exercise that put the scholar at the center, named the whole research life cycle: planning and data management plans, acquisition, storage, cleaning, analysis, publication, curation, and preservation.8 In practice that meant consultations on data cleaning, visualization, coding, and methods such as text and cluster analysis. As the team’s skills grew it added GIS, 3D and drone capture, machine learning, research software, reproducible workflows, and open-source licensing.

Not all of it happened one-on-one. The team ran Tidy Tuesday, a weekly social data-cleaning lab; judged an inaugural datathon with an Anderson student group in spring 2020; and helped program a UC-wide Love Data Week of more than 25 workshops and events. It taught inside degree programs too: the Sustainable LA Grand Challenge undergraduate scholars, the Mellon Mays fellows, and graduate programs in social science, urban planning, applied statistics, and management.9

The volume is documented for the later years. From 2023 to 2025 the unit logged at least 1,175 service interactions: 785 scheduled consultations, 156 service-desk tickets, and 234 tracked project collaborations. The School of Public Affairs, the Social Sciences division, and Life Sciences brought the most consultations. Over the same years the work changed shape, from single appointments toward project support that ran for months, and the longest of those engagements are in Research impact.10

Governed computation: Redivis and the L2 voter file

Before this, UCLA had no campus service for restricted data of this class outside Mednet, the medical enterprise’s computing environment (with HIPAA-level services such as a HIPAA-compliant Box), which the rest of campus could not use. There was no governed way to give a student access to a voter file, and no secure place outside medicine to merge FERPA-, PII-, or PHI-restricted data at scale (author’s account, 2026-09-27). To support that class of data, DSC selected and licensed Redivis, ran the institutional compliance review (Third-Party Risk Management, P3/P4 approval), negotiated access to the restricted datasets, piloted the platform in 2022, and launched the governed service in January 2023. It set up a 50-seat model with data-use-agreement controls so that routine requests would not pile up on one person.11

The main case is the L2 historic voter file, more than 10 TB of national voter history bought through the archive’s gift fund. It arrived as thousands of fragmented state-level files, too large to store locally and too inconsistent to analyze. DSC pulled it into a single version-controlled national dataset, published with its own DOI (UCLA Library, L2 Voter History and Demographic Dataset, 2023, doi:10.60603/D37P4R) and presented at FORCE11 2024 by Redivis’s CEO alongside the doctoral student who curated it, Graham Straus. Straus put the institutional point plainly: “What happens when I leave UCLA? Will people know how the dataset was constructed? Yes!” Researchers use it directly across departments, in election administration, gentrification, Latino voting, and a project merging the voter file with UCLA medical records to study health and voting. Recorded processing on the platform grew from 444 GB in its first year to 2.9 PB in 2024; those figures measure platform activity, not data held (holdings are about 20 TB). DSC provided the environment and the access. The scholarship stayed with the researchers. The point of it is not an “AI” story. It is that stewardship came to include governed computation, not only acquiring and describing open files.

Infrastructure: running it in the cloud

Almost none of this ran on hardware DSC owned. UCLA Dataverse runs on Amazon Web Services, with its files in S3, about 5.4 TiB counting replication as of May 2025. The point-cloud service described below runs on its own AWS server with about 4.5 TiB of storage, shared with the LARIAC imagery. Redivis is a licensed platform, not a local system. The exceptions were a deep-learning workstation in the Young Research Library, bought with a Powell gift; the 3D workstation and drone kit; and an R server DSC hosted for research groups whose data outgrew their own machines, Carceral Ecologies among them.

Paying for it took a sequence of moves. Dataverse first ran under an AWS account belonging to the Library’s IT division. When campus consolidated its AWS accounts, the cost landed on DSC. Powell infrastructure funds covered it until they ran out, and then the archive’s own gift fund took over. That last step depended on a budget change made earlier. DSC moved the archive’s large recurring subscriptions, ICPSR and Roper, off its own gift fund and onto the Library’s general membership budget, the way research libraries usually treat major database memberships. That freed the archive’s original fund to cover the cloud costs of Dataverse and the point-cloud platform. The move was possible because one role could see the archive’s budget and the infrastructure budget at the same time.12

Keeping the systems current was its own work. By 2025 production ran Dataverse 5.14, a release line upstream no longer patches, and DSC had begun rebuilding the service as code: Terraform for the AWS resources, an Ansible playbook forked from the Dataverse community’s, and a managed Postgres database, with the 6.x series as the target. That rebuild was not finished where this history stops.

Teaching: The Carpentries and an instructor network

Instruction went through three distinct eras, and the shift between them was reactive, not planned. With two staff and tens of thousands of potential learners, the unit joined The Carpentries in 2017 and used the membership to build an instructor community rather than hire one; Tim founded UCLA Carpentries in 2018 and spent its first two years teaching locally, one campus, in person. Between October 2017 and January 2020 the program ran six Software and Data Carpentry workshops on campus, in R and Python, at the Young Research Library, Powell Library, the IDRE Portal, the Mathematical Sciences building, and the LA Kretz Garden Pavilion for Ecology and Evolutionary Biology, alongside Library Carpentry workshops for librarians and information-studies students in 2017, 2018, and 2019. The instructor rosters show the model working: by the two January 2020 workshops, campus volunteers trained through the program were leading the teaching and DSC staff were mostly helping. COVID changed the economics of that model overnight: travel-based cross-campus training became impossible, but virtual delivery made cross-campus coordination suddenly cheap. Tim co-founded the systemwide UC Carpentries network in 2020 in direct response to that constraint, not as a planned expansion, with Berkeley, San Diego, Santa Barbara, Merced, and Riverside as partner campuses. What followed was sustained, not temporary: nine workshops in September 2024 alone drew 937 attendances across UCLA and partner UC campuses, and the 2025 series drew 558 attendees (139 from UCLA) across another nine sessions. The unit had gone from teaching its own campus to running system-scale infrastructure other campuses scheduled their own programming around, a change in kind, not just volume.

The Carpentries model mattered as much for who taught as for what was taught. The DSC adopted a Code of Conduct adapted from the Carpentries’ own. Every member of the DSC team trained as a Carpentries instructor, and by 2021 the program had added 13 active instructors, six of them from technical roles elsewhere in the Library: programmers, a digital asset coordinator, a technical product owner. Jamison became a regular lead instructor for the Unix shell in the UC-wide series and wrote a new lesson on Rclone for moving research data. Teaching gave those staff a reason to share what they knew, and gave DSC instructors it could never have hired. Library Carpentry lessons taught librarians and library-school students the same data and coding skills, which made the Library’s public-facing staff better partners on data-heavy research.13

What was taught followed demand. GIS was the largest single category every year, and Geography the largest single departmental audience. Qualitative and text-data analysis was a steady line, as were data management plans for the NIH data-sharing policy, high-performance computing, visualization, and 3D data. Machine learning and AI grew from a handful of sessions in 2023.

Tim’s own Carpentries work went past UCLA. An Instructor Trainer since 2016, he received the Carpentries’ Community Service Award in 2018, served on the inaugural Trainers Leadership Committee and co-wrote its bylaws, and edited a full revision of the Instructor Training curriculum. As chair of Library Carpentry’s Curriculum Advisory Committee from 2022 he moved six lessons into the official worldwide curriculum, among them DMP 101, a data management plans lesson aligned to the NIH data-sharing policy. He later co-chaired the Library Carpentry Governance Group, which sets lesson-adoption standards for the global program.

Open science and software curation

The IMLS grant RE-252335-OLS-22 ($237,839, Tim as Project Director)14 treated course material itself as a citable research output: 15 peer-reviewed open-science lessons against 14 proposed, each with a Zenodo DOI, CRediT contributor roles, ORCID identifiers, and Citation File Format metadata, built by 27 authors across 14 teams. Todd Grappone was Co-PI and Zhiyuan Yao, DSC’s spatial data science librarian, coordinated the program; Tim wrote the computational thinking and R lessons and rewrote the Python one. It is the clearest case of the software-curation and open-science strands in this era, and, because a federal award can be checked without anyone’s account of it, the one with the firmest outside evidence.

Tim is also Co-PI on the $1.85M Sloan Foundation grant that established the UC OSPO Network, a six-campus effort to treat open-source software as real scholarly infrastructure. That grant funds the network, not DSC. What DSC got out of it is a local UCLA open-source program office, the lead on the network’s education work, and shared services (licensing guidance, a repository browser, a summer student research program). The need is concrete: a 2025 scan of about 81,000 UC-affiliated code repositories found that 77.5% carried no license at all.

The open-science work also ran through the field’s own organizations. Tim has been a Center for Open Science ambassador since 2016 and taught in the Berkeley Initiative for Transparency in the Social Sciences’ Research Transparency and Reproducibility Training, and the Library held a Center for Open Science institutional membership in 2019–20. DSC staff maintain or co-maintain Carpentries lessons taught worldwide, from the Unix shell and R to newer lessons on qualitative data, Wikidata, and using AI coding agents in research without giving up validation and reproducibility.

Spatial data and GIS

Geography has consistently been the unit’s largest single workshop audience. Before the GIS position was cut in 2024, DSC’s geospatial librarian handled 70 to 80 booked consultations a year, started the systemwide UC GIS Week, ran UC GIS workshops that reached hundreds of people, managed the LARIAC regional aerial-imagery holdings (bought through the archive’s gift fund), and worked with UC San Diego and UC Santa Barbara on geospatial discovery for Dataverse. DSC also ran UCLA’s campus ArcGIS Online platform. It grew to more than 7,000 accounts and over 3,000 active users a year, and since the position was cut it has run past its licensed storage with no assigned administrator.15

Emerging technology: 3D capture, drones, and Potree

When the DSC planned its growth in 2018, it named three areas to build toward as it added staff: GIS, augmented and virtual reality, and machine learning.16 The second came through Doug Daniels, whose own career crosses the 2018 line. He joined the Library in 2013 on the help desk, built its first 3D printing service as a technical projects specialist, and from 2016 ran the Lux Lab, the Library’s emerging-technology space for 3D printing and scanning, laser cutting, large-format printing, and VR. In April 2020, after finishing his MLIS, he joined DSC as Emerging Technologies Librarian.17 His first months in the role were the pandemic’s, and the lab’s printers went to producing protective equipment.

The capture work came first. Daniels scanned objects for partners across campus and beyond: Egyptian artifacts at the Getty and the Museo Egizio in Turin with the Cotsen Institute (2018), cuneiform tablets in Library Special Collections, Fowler Museum objects, sarcophagus lids at LACMA, and Scripps’s research vessel FLIP (2021). Medicine became a steady partner: cadaver-arm scans across dissection stages with the Anatomy Lab (2022), now taught in History of Medicine; eye tissue scanned for machine-learning measurement at the Stein Eye Institute; thyroid specimens for Endocrinology (2025); and a 3D-printed surgical probe holder, built with an undergraduate, now in clinical use at Ronald Reagan UCLA Medical Center. The Easter Island Moai scans with Jo Anne Van Tilburg (2022) became a chapter in the Cotsen Institute’s illustrated atlas of the statues.

In 2023 DSC added a field kit: a drone with LiDAR and photogrammetry payloads, an RTK base station for survey-grade positioning, and a dedicated 3D workstation, flown by Daniels under an FAA remote-pilot certificate. It went to Iceland’s Mosfell Valley with a Cotsen doctoral student for a valley-scale survey (one trip, 2024, per Tim 2026-09-02; the DSC Statistics claim register does not yet approve a year, see Open questions), and to ongoing temple mapping at Angkor. It also went, twice, to Powell Library: a terrestrial LiDAR scan in 2021 and a drone survey in 2024, of the building named for the family whose gifts funded much of DSC’s growth.

Capture raised the archive’s oldest question in a new form: once the object is recorded, how does anyone else use it? A point cloud from a survey is usually too large to open as a single file in a browser or to hand out from a download page. DSC’s answer was pointcloud.ucla.edu, a service built on the open-source Potree viewer, which streams large point clouds so they can be explored interactively on the web. Lighter models went to the Lux Lab’s Sketchfab collection. The work stayed tied to standards. Daniels chairs the UC 3D Data Common Knowledge Group (a member from 2020, chair from 2024), which built the ALA-funded 3D Atlas; co-chairs the international IIIF 3D Community Group; and belongs to the IMLS-funded Community Standards for 3D Preservation effort.

Students carried a share of it. The Lux Lab had its own student line until 2024, when its funding became restricted and it merged into DataSquad. The best-known result is the Refugee Material Culture Initiative: with Professor Kelly Nguyen and the Vietnamese Heritage Museum, Daniels 3D scanned the “Boat of Hope,” a river sampan that carried nine refugees out of Vietnam in 1984, and prepared the models for the museum’s public seven-day narrative of one family’s escape; DataSquad student Bianca Badajos helped set up the Potree viewer that hosts it (credit per the DSC Statistics claim register, 2026-09-27). Daniels was named a UCLA Librarian of the Year for 2026, one of two given university-wide by LAUC-LA.18 His work also ran back to the donor relationship: he showed the lab to the Powell Society donor circle at open houses in 2017, 2018, and 2019.

Text, OCR, and machine learning

Machine learning was one of the three areas the DSC planned to grow into in 2018, and it arrived the way most of the unit’s capacity did, through people rather than a new department. Kristian Allen, a Digital Library programmer with a machine-learning background, joined at half time in 2018. Leigh Phan, on the team from 2017, started as a programmer; her early traces in the record are web maps for the archive’s site in 2018. In 2021 her job was rewritten around computational research, with machine learning and AI as its main line, and she became the DSC’s data scientist. A Powell gift paid for a deep-learning workstation in the Young Research Library, the unit’s first local GPU capacity. Phan ran it, loaded it with open-source models researchers could use locally, and when water damage ended the first machine, led the choice of its replacement.19

Text came first as teaching. A line of workshops on qualitative and text data, on cleaning and coding text for qualitative analysis and on open tools for qualitative methods, drew steadily from 2021 on. In consulting it showed up as individual projects: comparing Ming dynasty Buddhist texts with natural-language-processing methods for a graduate student in Asian Languages, or building machine-learning models in R to classify animal behavior from accelerometer data for a doctoral student in Ecology and Evolutionary Biology.20 Ethics came along with the methods. Phan and Ibraheem Ali, a sciences data librarian elsewhere in the Library who also taught in the Carpentries program, organized cross-UC data ethics workshops for UC Love Data Week in 2021 and 2022 with colleagues at UC San Diego and UC Berkeley, and published the model.21

OCR is where the archive’s old job met the new tools most directly. A great deal of research evidence sits in scanned images no one can search or count: handwritten tables, printed statistical series, text in scripts ordinary OCR software reads badly. In the unit’s later years Phan led DSC’s work on getting computable text out of them, printed and handwritten alike, and on transcribing recorded speech across languages. The projects came from researchers. For scanned Japanese documents about atomic-bomb survivors, the team compared six tools to find one that could read dense historical Japanese. Handwritten nineteenth-century German tables for a History professor, and handwritten German memoirs for a Sociology professor, defeated standard OCR and needed a different route. The 1976 Iranian National Census sat locked in scanned PDFs. A DataSquad student, Lukas Hager, built the core OCR pipeline those projects shared, and other students containerized a layout-analysis workflow for medieval canon-law texts. By 2026 the team had a triage tool that runs a fast baseline engine and a stronger AI model side by side and recommends the cheapest defensible path to usable text for a given collection.22 The same methods reached audio: transcribing interviews with dance scholars for World Arts and Cultures, and processing recorded conversations for a dissertation in speech research.

The team’s approach to AI followed the archive’s older instincts about stewardship. Where it could, DSC ran open models on its own hardware, so sensitive research material did not leave UCLA and researchers were not tied to a commercial service to read their own sources. AI and machine learning also became a teaching line of its own, growing from a handful of sessions in 2023, and DSC staff published a Carpentries-style lesson on using AI coding agents in research while keeping the work validated and reproducible.

Phan’s later work carried the same habits into research software. After training at the INTERSECT research-software-engineering bootcamp in 2025, she helped write a lesson on building better research software, co-taught a UC Love Data Week 2026 workshop on making research software citable and discoverable (doi:10.5281/zenodo.18615247), and became co-lead of UCLA’s open source program office.

DataSquad: a student research workforce

DataSquad borrowed its model from Carleton College, where a student-staffed data consulting program had been given its own identity and structure.23 UCLA’s version began in 2020 on a Norman Powell gift that paid the first cohort’s salaries and laptops, and became a formal program in 2021 under Tim; Leigh Phan became co-director in 2025. The Library added funds in 2023 to expand the team and add in-person hours. In 2024 it absorbed the Lux Lab student line, and from 2025 a single cohort of six was cross-trained to cover data consulting and 3D work alike.

Students worked with staff, not in front of them, and senior students ran project management. Their share grew as staff capacity shrank. DataSquad students handled 190 of the unit’s scheduled consultations from 2023 to 2025, about a quarter, most of them in 2023. After that their work moved toward sustained project support, where tracked tasks grew from 6 in 2023 to 111 in 2025, as departments started planning research around the team’s availability rather than calling only when something broke. Two long engagements show the kind of work. Lawrence Lee and Madeline Kim rebuilt the pipeline for Carceral Ecologies’ helicopter-surveillance data so that a load that took about 318 minutes ran in about two. Julia Wood, Ethan Allavarpu, Tristan Dewing, and Lee cleaned and restructured the BioCritical Studies Lab’s national deaths-in-custody data, cutting 41,466 records to 29,208 without losing what the lab needed, and built tools the lab could maintain on its own.

It was also a pipeline. Of the ten students whose outcomes after graduation were tracked for 2023 to 2025, all went into data or technical roles, with McKinsey, Amazon, AIG, Stanford, and CVS Health among the destinations; Julia Wood joined the Library as a data specialist. The model has been picked up elsewhere through a DataSquad International network and written up in a co-authored chapter in ACRL’s Data Culture. Its research contributions are in the spotlights under Research impact.

People and funding

Much of this era’s expansion ran on Norman Powell’s philanthropy: a data-scientist position, a deep-learning machine and DSC space in the Young Research Library, infrastructure funds that carried Dataverse’s cloud costs for a time, and DataSquad’s seed funding, all separate from his dated $5M December 2016 gift that endowed the University Librarian position.

The people who carried the work were Jamie Jamison (repository and curation), Doug Daniels (3D and emerging technology), Leigh Phan (programmer to data scientist, computational research and AI), Zhiyuan Yao (spatial data science, until 2024), Kristian Allen (software and machine learning, 2018–2024), a data-science facilitator, and the rotating DataSquad students. Open discrepancies not yet resolved

  • 1977 is not a founding. Framing mostly settled. Tim, Stephenson’s own Dataverse-page text, and Stiles & Stephenson 2008 all agree: the archive function ran continuously from the 1960s (PBA, then the SRC’s Archive Data Library, “reference-room basis only”); 1977 is when Treiman designed the research-support framework, the unit was named SSDA, and a Data Archivist line and consultative services were added. The sources that “disagree” describe different facets: Stephenson credits Treiman and omits ISSR; the 2017 decks and the OECD directory name ISSR; the published book chapter24 says “part of the Survey Research Center” (which by 1977 was a component of ISSR). Still to check documentarily: whether Treiman’s 1977 framework reported directly to ISSR or to SRC-within-ISSR. Confirm against the 1977–78 UCLA General Catalog.
  • The 1964 SRC arrangement and Eve Fielder: Stephenson’s Dataverse-page text says the SRC formed in 1964 “under the direction of Eve Fielder” and the PBA merged into its planned Archive Data Library. A published obituary (LA Times, via legacy.com) says Fielder became SRC’s first director only in 1976, that SRC moved in 2004 to a Health Services Research division in UCLA General Internal Medicine, and that she was associate director there until retiring in 2006. Either SRC ran from 1964 under someone else, or the account compresses two events. Not resolved against SRC’s own records.
  • The ISSR-era director list and three-subperiod structure (spot-verified 2026-09-02): Sears’s ISSR directorship 1993–2008 is independently confirmed (Wikipedia, UCLA psychology page). The three subperiods are grounded in the digitized UCLA General Catalogs (evidence/deep-research-report.md cites 2003–05 through 2013–14). Freeman’s term (1974–1981) and Brewer’s end (1993, when she left for Ohio State and Sears took over) are firm; still open: Brewer’s start year, any gap 1981–1983, and whether the ISSR-to-ISR rename had a formal date/authority (none found).
  • ISSR-to-ISR rename and ISR dissolution: no date or authority found for either; no director identified for the institute under the ISR name (~2009–2011).
  • The “8 founding members” of ICPSR, and “through his efforts”: both phrasings are from Stephenson’s Dataverse-page text, the archive’s institutional memory, not an independent record. UCLA’s founding-member status is primary-verified (Miller’s 1962 memo). The “8” is not, Austin’s list has 22, Miller’s 21; “8” may be the “Committee of Eight” (1962–63 correspondence in ICPSR’s Bentley archive), unread. Marvick’s personal founding role is undocumented (see the 1961–1964 section).
  • UC Carpentries workshop/learner counts: Tim’s SOPA states “28 workshops… 2,896 learners” for the 2023-2025 review period; his Data Summary states “37 workshops (2022–2025)… 1,495 attendees (2024–2025)” for what may be a different scope (UC Carpentries alone vs. UCLA Carpentries + UC Carpentries combined) and a different date window. The founding/co-founding distinction (UCLA Carpentries 2018, UC Carpentries co-founded 2020) is resolved per Tim 2026-08-09; the workshop counts are not. Use the SOPA’s explicitly-scoped figure if one number is needed publicly.
  • Exact dates for three of Norman Powell’s four gifts (data scientist position, deep learning machine/DSC space, DataSquad seed funding), all undated in every source checked.
  • “The first reorg”: Tim mentioned a first reorganization during the 2018-present span that reduced data-science support capacity. Recorded as a bare fact per the Method/independence note’s discipline; not dated or elaborated, deliberately kept out of the public narrative entirely, not just trimmed, since this project’s whole premise is staying independent of any reorg argument (see history.qmd Methods and limitations).
  • Jamie Jamison’s DB dump / staff dates for 1977-1986, tracked in ucla-data-science-center/dataverse-infrastructure#10, not yet received.
  • The Marvick Papers (UCLA Special Collections, 63 boxes, 1955-1998), not yet consulted for primary founding documents, including Marvick’s own 1967 proposal.
  • Tim’s own 2021 book chapter, “From a Data Archive to Data Science” (Dennis et al., in Herndon ed., Data Science in the Library, Facet, pp.99-109), not yet read; likely already contains a considered account of the 2014-2018 transition that predates and could sharpen everything reconstructed here piecemeal.
